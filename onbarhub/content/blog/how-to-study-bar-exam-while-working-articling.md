The image of bar exam preparation, long uninterrupted days of study, is a fantasy for most candidates. The reality is that you are probably articling, working, or managing other responsibilities, and you need to fit preparation into the gaps. This is entirely doable. Most people who pass did exactly that. But it requires a different approach than the full-time-study fantasy, built around consistency and efficiency rather than volume. Here is how.

## Accept the Real Constraint

Start by being honest about your actual available time. If you work or article full-time, you do not have eight-hour study days on weekdays, and pretending you do leads to a plan you cannot follow and the guilt spiral that comes with falling behind it.

What you do have, realistically:

- A limited weekday window (early morning or evening)
- Larger weekend blocks
- The occasional day off

Plan around what you actually have, not what you wish you had. A plan built on real availability is one you can keep. A plan built on fantasy availability collapses in week two.

## Consistency Beats Intensity

The most important principle for working candidates: **a steady weekly rhythm over a longer runway beats sporadic heavy sessions.**

A candidate who studies two focused hours most weekday evenings and a solid block each weekend, sustained over two to three months, covers more ground and retains more than one who does nothing for weeks then attempts brutal fourteen-hour weekend crams. The steady approach also builds the material into long-term memory through spaced repetition, while cramming loads it into short-term memory that fades.

Set a weekly target you can realistically hit, perhaps in the range of 12 to 20 focused hours depending on your runway, and protect it. Hitting a sustainable target most weeks compounds. Missing an unsustainable one most weeks demoralizes.

## Protect a Consistent Slot

Decide when you study and defend that time. The specific slot matters less than its consistency.

Some candidates study early morning before work, when they are fresh and nothing has eaten the time yet. Others use a fixed evening block. Either works. What does not work is "whenever I get a chance," because chances do not arrive on their own, they get consumed by everything else.

Pick a slot you can realistically repeat, put it in your calendar, and treat it as an appointment you do not cancel. The best study time is the one you will actually keep, not the one that is theoretically optimal.

## Use Efficient Methods

When study time is scarce, efficiency matters even more. Passive rereading is the least efficient method and the easiest to default to when you are tired. Lean instead on methods that do more per hour:

**Practice questions** are the highest-value activity. They reveal what you do not know, train retrieval, and show you where the exam concentrates. For a time-pressed candidate, an hour of practice questions is worth more than two hours of rereading.

**Index-building** doubles as study. As you build your navigation system, you engage with the material in a way that sticks, and you produce the tool you need on exam day. Two outcomes from one block of time.

**Targeted review** of your weak areas, identified through practice, beats rereading everything evenly. Spend your limited hours where they move the needle.

## Structure the Runway

A workable shape for a working candidate over roughly ten weeks:

**Early weeks:** Fit a first pass through the material into your weekday slots and weekend blocks. Build your index as you go. Keep moving; do not get stuck trying to master everything on the first read.

**Middle weeks:** Shift weekend blocks toward practice questions. Use weekday slots for targeted review of weak areas the questions reveal. Refine your index.

**Final weeks:** Prioritize timed practice on weekends with your real materials and index. Use weekday slots to firm up specific weaknesses. Taper slightly in the last few days so you arrive rested rather than depleted.

Adjust the length to your runway, but keep the arc: understand, then practice, then simulate.

## Guard Against Burnout

Studying on top of full-time work is draining, and burnout is a real risk that sabotages more candidates than lack of ability. Protect against it:

**Keep sessions focused, not marathon.** Two genuinely focused hours beat four distracted ones. When your attention is gone, stop; tired rereading is near-worthless.

**Build in rest.** Schedule at least some downtime. A completely unbroken grind for months degrades your performance and your mood. Rest is part of the plan, not a failure of it.

**Use the efficiency of active methods** so you get more from less time and do not need to grind endlessly.

**Remember it is temporary.** A defined runway of a couple of months is survivable precisely because it ends. Keep the finish line in view.

## Communicate and Protect Your Time

If you are articling or working, where appropriate and possible, let the relevant people know you are preparing for a licensing exam. Many workplaces understand and can offer some flexibility around the exam period. You will not always get accommodation, but you cannot get it if no one knows. And protect your study slot from the social and domestic commitments that otherwise fill every hour; a temporary, explained reduction in availability is reasonable during exam preparation.

## The Bottom Line

Studying for the LSO exams while working or articling is the normal path, not a disadvantage to lament. It works when you plan around your real available time, prioritize consistency over intensity, protect a repeatable study slot, use efficient active methods over passive rereading, and guard against burnout with built-in rest. A sustainable rhythm sustained over a couple of months, built from the hours you actually have, carries most working candidates across the line.
