On the LSO exams, the hard part is rarely a question where you have no idea. It is the question where two options both look right. This guide gives you a repeatable way to handle those, so you stop losing marks you actually know.

## Why This Happens on Purpose

The exam writers are not trying to trick you with silly wrong answers. They are testing whether you can tell a precise right answer from a plausible near miss. So the wrong options are usually one of a few types:

- **True, but not responsive.** The statement is correct in general, but it does not answer the exact question asked.
- **Right in a different situation.** It would be the answer if one fact in the scenario were different.
- **Almost right.** Correct except for a single word, number, or condition.
- **The common misconception.** The answer most people would give from instinct, which the rule actually rejects.

Once you expect these, you stop being surprised by them and start hunting for the difference.

## Read the Question Before the Options

Read the stem carefully and decide what it is actually asking before you look at the choices. Candidates lose marks by reading the scenario, jumping to the options, and then picking the one that sounds familiar.

Ask yourself: what is the precise thing being tested here? Is it a deadline, a threshold, who owes a duty, which document applies, whether something is permitted or required? Fix that in your mind first. Then the options have to earn their place against that specific question.

## The Two-Option Method

When you have narrowed it to two, do not stare and hope. Do this instead:

**Name the difference.** The two surviving options almost always differ on one specific thing. Say it out loud in your head: "A says the limit is 30 days, B says 60." The whole question now turns on that one point.

**Go to that one point in your materials.** You are allowed your materials, so use them for the deciding detail, not to reread the whole chapter. Find the sentence that settles the single difference you named.

**Check the exact word.** Permitted or required. May or must. Shall or should. These small words decide more bar exam questions than any big concept.

If you have no time to search, choose the option that answers the exact question asked, not the one that is merely a true statement.

## Watch for the Reversers: NOT and EXCEPT

A question that asks which option is NOT correct, or which is correct EXCEPT one, flips everything. You are now looking for the odd one out. Underline the word the moment you see it, and when you evaluate each option, ask "is this the false one" rather than "is this true."

These are some of the most reliably lost marks on the exam, and they are lost to speed, not to knowledge. Slow down by a few seconds and you keep them.

## Trust Your First Read

Unless you find a concrete reason to change, keep your first answer. A concrete reason is finding the rule, or realizing you misread a fact. A vague feeling that another option "might be better" is not a reason, and acting on it tends to cost more marks than it saves. Your review data will usually show this: track how often changing an answer turned a right into a wrong, and let that teach you.

## Pace, Flag, Move

If a question is eating your time, give it your best current answer, flag it, and move on. A question you spend five minutes on is worth the same single mark as one you answer in forty seconds, and the time you burn comes straight out of questions you could have banked. Come back to flagged questions at the end with whatever time remains.

## Answer Everything

On an exam with no stated penalty for a wrong answer, a blank is a guaranteed zero. Before time is called, make sure every question has something filled in, even a quick best guess. Managing your pace so you are never forced to leave a block of questions blank is itself part of scoring well.

## Practising the Skill

This is a trainable skill, not a talent. You get better at it by doing timed questions and then reviewing not just whether you were right, but why the wrong options were wrong. That is where the pattern recognition comes from. Over enough questions, the near misses start to look obvious, because you have seen their shape before.

## The Bottom Line

The bar exam rewards precision, not recall. Read the question before the options, name the single difference between the two that survive, settle it on the exact word rather than the general idea, respect NOT and EXCEPT, keep your first answer unless you have a real reason to change, and never leave a blank. Do that consistently and the two-answers-look-right problem stops being the thing that sinks you.
