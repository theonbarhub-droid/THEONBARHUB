Candidates understandably want to know exactly how the LSO exams are scored and precisely what they need to pass. The honest answer involves more uncertainty than people like, because the LSO does not hand candidates a simple fixed cutoff to aim at. This guide explains what is known, what is not, and why the uncertainty should change how you prepare rather than worry you.

## Pass or Fail, Not a Percentage You're Handed

The LSO reports results as pass or fail. Candidates generally do not receive a detailed score breakdown or a per-question report. You find out whether you cleared the standard, not the exact margin.

This frustrates candidates who want to know "how close was I?" But for your preparation, the pass-or-fail framing is actually clarifying: your job is to get comfortably over the line, not to calculate the minimum.

## How the Pass Mark Is Set

The LSO uses an established standard-setting process to determine the passing standard for each administration of the exam. The important implications for you:

**It is not a fixed public percentage you can assume.** You should not go in thinking "I need exactly X percent" based on a number you read somewhere. The standard is set by the LSO's process, and you cannot assume a specific cutoff in advance.

**It reflects a competence standard.** Standard-setting processes aim to identify the level of performance that represents the competence the exam is meant to measure. The standard is about whether you are ready to practise, not about ranking you against other candidates for a fixed number of seats.

Because the precise cutoff is not something you can count on in advance, the rational strategy is to prepare to perform comfortably above any plausible passing standard. Aim for mastery, not for the minimum.

## Why Chasing a Precise Cutoff Backfires

Some candidates fixate on a target number and prepare to just reach it. This is risky for several reasons:

**You do not know the exact standard in advance.** Preparing to scrape a number you are guessing at leaves no margin if the standard or your performance differs from your assumption.

**Exam-day variance is real.** Nerves, a tricky question cluster, or time pressure can cost you several questions you would get right in calmer conditions. A margin absorbs that variance. A minimalist target does not.

**Time pressure compounds.** On a tightly timed exam, aiming to answer only enough to pass means any slowdown, a hard-to-find rule, a slow start, can drop you below the line. Aiming high gives you room.

The candidates who pass comfortably are not the ones who calculated the minimum. They are the ones who prepared to be well over it, so that even a bad day still clears the bar.

## Answer Everything

On a multiple-choice exam without a stated penalty for wrong answers, a blank guarantees zero on that question while a guess has a real chance of being right. The math favours answering every question.

Confirm the current rules in your official LSO instructions, but the general principle holds: do not leave blanks. If you are running low on time, make sure every question has an answer, even a quick best guess, before time is called. Manage your time so you are never forced to leave a block of questions blank.

## Time Management Is Part of Scoring

Because the exams are tightly timed and open-material, your score is shaped not just by what you know but by how fast you can find and apply it. Two candidates with identical knowledge can get very different scores if one navigates their materials quickly and the other searches slowly.

This is why your index and your practice under timed conditions matter so much. They are not separate from scoring. They directly determine how many questions you can actually answer well in the time allowed.

## What This Means for Your Preparation

**Do not target a specific number.** You cannot reliably know the cutoff in advance. Target solid mastery.

**Build a margin.** Prepare to perform well above any plausible passing standard so exam-day variance does not sink you.

**Answer every question.** No blanks on an exam without a stated wrong-answer penalty.

**Train speed, not just knowledge.** Your score depends on retrieval speed under time pressure, which comes from a strong index and timed practice.

**Stop worrying about the exact cutoff.** It is not knowable in advance and it is not the useful thing to focus on. Preparation you can control; the cutoff you cannot.

## The Bottom Line

The LSO sets the passing standard through a standard-setting process, reports results as pass or fail, and does not hand candidates a fixed cutoff to aim at. The right response is not anxiety about the unknown number but a shift in strategy: prepare to clear any plausible standard comfortably, build a margin against exam-day variance, answer every question, and train the navigation speed that determines how much of your knowledge you can actually deploy in the time allowed.
