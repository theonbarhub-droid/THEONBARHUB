If you trained as a lawyer outside Canada, the road to practising in Ontario has two big stages that are easy to confuse: the NCA, and then the Law Society of Ontario licensing process. This guide lays out the whole journey in order, in plain language, so you can see where you are and what comes next.

## The Two Stages, Kept Separate

The single most useful thing to understand early is that these are two different systems run by two different bodies.

The **NCA**, the National Committee on Accreditation, is run by the Federation of Law Societies of Canada. Its job is to assess your legal education and experience from another country and confirm it meets Canadian standards. When you finish, you receive a Certificate of Qualification.

The **LSO licensing process** is run by the Law Society of Ontario. This is where you actually become licensed to practise in the province. It includes the bar examinations and the experiential training requirement, and it ends with your call to the bar.

You generally finish the NCA stage first and then enter the LSO process. Keep the two straight in your head, because the deadlines, the fees, and the people you contact are different for each.

## Stage One: The NCA

The NCA reviews your file and tells you what you need to do to meet Canadian standards. For many internationally trained lawyers that means completing a set of subject assessments, and often a legal research and writing requirement, before you receive your certificate.

The exact requirements are individual. Two people from the same country and the same law school can get different assessments depending on their transcripts and experience. Work from your own NCA assessment, not from what a friend was told to do.

When you have met every requirement on your assessment, the NCA issues your Certificate of Qualification. That certificate is your key into the provincial licensing process.

## Stage Two: Entering the LSO Process

With your NCA certificate, you apply to the Law Society of Ontario to begin licensing. This is an application with its own eligibility rules, fees and deadlines, and it is the gate you pass through before you can register for the exams.

Give this step real attention. Missing a licensing application deadline can cost you a whole sitting, because you usually cannot register for an exam until you have applied to the process and paid.

## The Bar Examinations

Ontario has two licensing examinations, the Barrister exam and the Solicitor exam. Both are open-book and multiple choice, and both are tightly timed. The Barrister exam covers areas like public law, criminal, civil litigation, family, and professional responsibility. The Solicitor exam covers areas like real estate, business law, estates and professional responsibility.

Here is the honest part that trips people up: your NCA studying helps with the underlying law, but it does not prepare you for the format of these exams. The bar exams are not about writing essays or recalling doctrine from memory. They are about finding and applying the right rule, fast, in materials you are allowed to bring in. That is a different skill, and it needs its own preparation, which is exactly what tools like practice exams and a strong index are for.

## The Experiential Training Requirement

Alongside the exams, Ontario requires experiential training, most commonly articling, though there are other routes. This is the supervised practical stage of licensing. The requirements, timing and any exemptions are set by the LSO, and this is an area where internationally trained candidates often have questions about their specific situation, so check the current rules for your circumstances.

## The Call to the Bar

Once you have passed the examinations and completed the experiential training and the other licensing requirements, you are called to the bar and licensed to practise law in Ontario. The call to the bar is the ceremony and the formal moment it becomes real.

## Where People Get Stuck

A few patterns come up again and again for people on this path:

**Treating the bar exams like NCA exams.** They are not. Build your preparation around speed, navigation and timed practice, not memorization.

**Losing time between stages.** The gap between finishing the NCA and registering for an exam is where deadlines get missed. Map the dates for your next intended sitting backward from the exam, and note the licensing application deadline, not just the exam date.

**Working from someone else's plan.** Your NCA assessment, your timing, and your experiential training situation are specific to you. Use official sources for your own facts.

## The Bottom Line

The journey is NCA first, then the LSO licensing process, which contains the exams, the experiential training, and finally the call to the bar. The two stages are run by different bodies with different rules, so keep them separate, work from your own assessment and the official sources, and remember that the bar exams are a new kind of test that rewards preparation built for their open-book, time-pressured format.
