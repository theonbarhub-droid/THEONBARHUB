"How many hours should I study?" is one of the first questions candidates ask about the LSO Solicitor exam. The honest answer is that the number varies a lot, and the number itself is less important than what you do with the time. But candidates want a target, so this guide gives realistic ranges and, more usefully, explains how to spend the hours well.

## The Realistic Range

There is no official study-hour requirement, and self-reported numbers vary widely because candidates come in with different backgrounds. That said, many candidates who pass report preparation in the range of roughly 150 to 250 hours spread over one to three months.

Take that range as a loose anchor, not a rule. Several factors move you within it or outside it:

**Your background.** A candidate who has done real estate or estates work will move through those sections faster than someone seeing the material for the first time. Transactional experience is a real advantage on the Solicitor exam.

**Your study efficiency.** Two hundred hours of passive rereading is worth less than one hundred hours of active practice. How you study changes how many hours you need.

**Your starting point.** If you are coming off a recent fail and already know the material's shape, your retake preparation can be more targeted and shorter than a first attempt.

Do not anchor on someone else's number. The candidate who says they studied eighty hours and passed may have five years of real estate practice. The one who studied three hundred may have been reading passively the whole time.

## Why Hours Are the Wrong Metric

Total hours is a tempting metric because it is easy to count. But it measures input, not progress. Two candidates can both put in two hundred hours and be in completely different places on exam day, because one practiced retrieval and the other reread chapters.

The Solicitor exam does not test whether you have seen the material. It tests whether you can **find and apply** the right rule under time pressure. That capacity comes from active practice, not accumulated reading time. So rather than targeting a number of hours, target the activities that actually build exam readiness.

## How to Spend the Hours

Divide your preparation across three activities, weighted toward the ones that produce results.

**Reading to understand (first pass).** Go through the material once to understand what is there and how it fits together. This builds the map. It is necessary but not sufficient, and candidates who stop here tend to underperform. Keep this pass moving; do not try to memorize on the first read.

**Building your index as you go.** As you finish each topic, index it. This does double duty: it forces engagement with the material and produces the navigation tool you need on exam day. Indexing is studying, not administration.

**Practicing questions under time pressure.** This is where most of your value comes from. Practice questions reveal the gap between "I read this" and "I can answer this." They train retrieval speed. They show you where the exam concentrates and where you are weak. Weight your hours here as you approach the exam.

A rough shape: early on, more reading and indexing; later, mostly practice questions and index refinement. By your final weeks you should be doing timed practice with your real materials and index, diagnosing weaknesses, and targeting them.

## A Workable Timeline

For most candidates, six to ten weeks of consistent preparation works well:

**Weeks 1 to 3:** First read-through, building the index topic by topic. Understand the structure. Start light practice.

**Weeks 4 to 6:** Shift toward practice questions. Identify weak areas. Refine the index where you struggled to find things.

**Final weeks:** Timed full-length practice with real materials. Target weaknesses. Tighten your index. Taper intensity in the last day or two so you arrive rested.

Starting earlier with lighter weekly hours and building toward the exam beats cramming everything into the last fortnight, which is the single most common preparation mistake.

## Quality Signals to Track

Instead of counting hours, track whether you are actually getting readier:

- Can you find a topic in your index in seconds, or are you still searching?
- Are your practice scores improving over time?
- Can you finish timed sections without running out of time?
- Do real estate closing steps and estate processes feel automatic, or do you still have to reason them out slowly?

These tell you more about your readiness than any hour count.

## The Bottom Line

If you need a target, aim for consistent preparation over six to ten weeks in the rough range of 150 to 250 hours, adjusted for your background. But treat the hours as a container, not a goal. Fill them with active practice and index-building rather than passive rereading, and track readiness by your ability to find and apply rules fast. A candidate who studies fewer hours the right way beats one who studies more hours the wrong way.
