The LSO Barrister and Solicitor exams are open-material. You can bring your printed study materials into the exam. New candidates often hear this and relax. That is a mistake.

The exams are also strictly timed, with more questions than you can comfortably answer if you have to search for each one. You will not read your materials during the exam. You will **find** answers, fast, or you will run out of time. The tool that lets you do that is your index.

## Why the Index Decides Your Day

Think about the actual mechanics of the exam. A question gives you a scenario and asks what a lawyer should do, or what a rule requires, or which deadline applies. You know the topic. You need the specific answer, which is somewhere in hundreds of pages of materials.

If you have to flip through looking for it, you lose a minute or more per question. Across a full exam, that is time you do not have. If you can turn to the right page in five seconds because your index tells you exactly where it is, you answer and move on.

The candidates who struggle are rarely the ones who did not know the material existed. They are the ones who could not find it in time.

## Organize by the Question, Not the Textbook

The single most important principle: **index the way questions are asked, not the way the materials are structured.**

The official materials are organized by subject area in a logical teaching order. Questions do not come at you that way. A question about a limitation period, a conflict of interest, or a closing step does not announce which chapter it comes from. It describes a situation.

So your index should map the language of problems to page numbers. When you read a practice question, ask: what words would I look up to find this? Index under those words.

Index the same concept under multiple entries if it could be searched different ways. A rule about client identification might be findable under "identification," "know your client," "retainer," and "money laundering." Cross-reference generously. On exam day you want to find the page no matter which word comes to mind first.

## Build It in Layers

A strong system usually has three layers:

**Tabs** for major sections. Physical tabs let you jump to the right area of the binder instantly. Keep these to the main divisions so you are not hunting through fifty tabs.

**A master index** that maps topics and keywords to exact page numbers. This is your primary tool. Alphabetical or grouped by theme, whichever you navigate faster. The test is speed, not elegance.

**Margin notes and highlights** on the pages themselves, so once you are on the right page you can see the key point immediately without rereading the whole section.

## Make the Index Itself a Study Tool

Here is the part candidates underuse: building the index **is studying.**

To index a topic well, you have to understand it well enough to know what matters, how it might be tested, and what words a question would use. The act of deciding where something goes and how to label it forces the kind of engagement that passive rereading never produces.

This is the main argument for building your own index rather than buying one. A purchased index is someone else's mental map. You can use it, but you will be slower, because you did not do the thinking that makes navigation instinctive. When you build it yourself, you remember roughly where things are even before you check, because you put them there.

## Test It Under Pressure

An index you have never used under time pressure is an untested tool. Do your mock exams with your actual materials and your actual index. You will discover:

- Entries you cannot find because you labelled them with words you do not naturally think of
- Topics you forgot to index at all
- Sections where your page numbers are wrong
- Where you are slow and need better tabs

Fix these before exam day. By your final mocks, your index should feel like an extension of your memory, not a document you consult.

## Start Early

A thorough index is not a last-week project. It takes many hours, and it needs refining as you learn where questions concentrate. Start as you study. Index each topic as you finish it. Then spend your final weeks testing and tightening the system rather than building it from scratch while also trying to review everything else.

## Common Mistakes

**Indexing by chapter title.** Your index should use the words of problems, not headings.

**Too few entries.** If a concept only appears once in your index under one word, you will miss it when a question uses a different word. Over-index.

**Never testing it.** An index you have not used in a timed mock is a guess, not a tool.

**Leaving it to the end.** Cramming the index into the final week means you neither index well nor review well.

**Pristine materials.** Materials with no tabs, no highlights, and no notes mean you are reading cold on exam day. Mark them up.

## The Bottom Line

The LSO exams reward preparation that most candidates underestimate: not just knowing the law, but being able to locate the exact rule under time pressure. Your index is the system that makes that possible. Build it yourself, build it early, organize it around how questions are framed, and test it in every mock. Do that and the open-material format becomes the advantage it is supposed to be.
