The path from law graduate to licensed Ontario lawyer runs through the Law Society of Ontario's licensing process. For internationally trained lawyers who have come through the NCA, and for graduates of Canadian law schools, the process has the same core components. This overview explains the pieces and how they fit together, so you can see the whole map before focusing on any one part.

## The Big Picture

To be licensed as a lawyer in Ontario, you generally need to:

1. Meet the academic requirement
2. Enter the LSO licensing process
3. Complete experiential training
4. Pass the Barrister and Solicitor licensing examinations
5. Satisfy the good character requirement
6. Be called to the bar

These do not all happen in a rigid sequence, and candidates often work on several in overlapping periods. Let us take them one at a time.

## 1. The Academic Requirement

Before entering the licensing process, you need the academic foundation:

**Canadian law graduates** satisfy this through their law degree from an accredited Canadian law school.

**Internationally trained lawyers** satisfy it through the National Committee on Accreditation (NCA) process, which assesses foreign legal credentials and requires passing NCA examinations to obtain a Certificate of Qualification. Once you hold the certificate, you are academically eligible to enter the LSO process.

## 2. Entering the Licensing Process

You formally enter the LSO licensing process by applying and paying the required fees within the LSO's application windows. The licensing cycle has set dates, and entering the process is what makes you a licensing candidate eligible to register for exams and experiential training.

## 3. Experiential Training

Every candidate must complete an experiential training requirement. There are two main routes:

**Articling.** A period of supervised legal work (commonly around ten months) with an approved articling principal or employer. This is the traditional route and remains the most common.

**The Law Practice Program (LPP).** A structured alternative that combines a training course with a work placement, designed for candidates who do not secure an articling position.

**Exemptions.** Some candidates, particularly those with substantial prior legal experience, apply to the LSO for an exemption from part or all of the experiential training requirement. The LSO assesses these requests individually, and an exemption is not guaranteed. If you have practised law elsewhere, this is worth exploring, but plan on the standard route unless and until an exemption is granted.

## 4. The Licensing Examinations

The two exams are the part most candidates worry about most:

**The Barrister exam** covers advocacy areas: public law, criminal, civil litigation, family, administrative law, and professional responsibility.

**The Solicitor exam** covers transactional areas: real estate, business law, wills and estates, and professional responsibility.

Both are multiple-choice, open-material, and strictly timed. They are written on separate dates and scored independently. You can write them in the same licensing cycle or in different cycles, and in either order.

Each exam is offered several times a year. The LSO publishes exam dates along with the application, accommodation, and registration deadlines for each sitting. These deadlines matter enormously, because the accommodation and registration deadlines for a given sitting often fall well before the exam and sometimes before the previous sitting's results are released.

## 5. The Good Character Requirement

Licensing includes a good character assessment. You disclose relevant background as part of your application, and the LSO assesses whether there are issues bearing on your suitability to be licensed. For most candidates this is a straightforward disclosure step. Where there are matters to address, the LSO has a process for considering them. Be honest and complete in your disclosures; non-disclosure is treated more seriously than most of the underlying matters.

## 6. Call to the Bar

Once you have completed experiential training, passed both exams, and satisfied all requirements, you are called to the bar at a formal ceremony. You take the oath of office, receive your license, and become a member of the LSO entitled to practise law in Ontario. The ceremony is scheduled after your results and administrative steps are confirmed, typically in the period following each exam cycle.

## How the Pieces Fit in Time

There is no single required order, but a common shape looks like this:

- Complete the academic requirement (law degree or NCA certificate)
- Enter the licensing process in an application window
- Line up experiential training (secure articling or enrol in the LPP)
- Write the licensing exams around the same broad period, in whatever order and cycle suits you
- Complete the good character step
- Be called to the bar once everything is done

Many candidates overlap exam preparation with articling. Others write exams first or space things out. The right sequence depends on your articling situation, your finances, and how you prepare best.

## The Deadlines That Trip People Up

The single most common administrative mistake is missing a deadline for the next exam sitting. Because accommodation and registration deadlines can fall before results are released, candidates who wait to see a result before planning their next step sometimes miss the window.

Keep a running view of the upcoming sittings and their deadlines. If you might need to write or rewrite an exam, know the next sitting's registration and accommodation deadlines before you need them.

## Where the Exams Fit in Your Overall Plan

The exams are the component you can most directly control through preparation, which is why they get so much attention. But they are one part of a larger process. Keep the whole map in view: the academic requirement, experiential training, the exams, good character, and the call. Missing an administrative step or deadline can delay you as surely as failing an exam.

## The Bottom Line

Becoming a lawyer in Ontario means working through the LSO licensing process: academic qualification, entry into the process, experiential training, two licensing exams, a good character assessment, and the Call to the Bar. The components overlap and offer some flexibility in order, but every one has deadlines and requirements set by the LSO. Understand the whole process, track the deadlines, and give the exams the focused preparation they demand, and the path from candidate to called lawyer becomes a sequence of manageable steps rather than a fog.
