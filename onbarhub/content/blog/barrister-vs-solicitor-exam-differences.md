Ontario's licensing process requires passing two separate exams: the Barrister exam and the Solicitor exam. They are not two halves of the same test. They cover different subject matter, have a different feel, and reward somewhat different preparation. Understanding the distinction helps you study efficiently and choose your order.

## The Basic Structure

Both exams share a format:

- Multiple-choice questions
- Open-material (you bring your own printed study materials)
- Strictly timed, with more questions than you can comfortably answer if you have to search for each one
- Scored on a pass or fail basis
- Written on separate dates

Both also test **professional responsibility**, which runs through everything a lawyer does and appears on both exams. This is one area where preparation carries over.

Beyond format and professional responsibility, the content diverges.

## The Barrister Exam

The Barrister exam covers the advocacy side of practice: the areas where lawyers appear before courts and tribunals and advise clients in disputes. Subject areas generally include:

- Public law
- Criminal law and procedure
- Civil litigation
- Family law
- Administrative law
- Professional responsibility and practice management

The feel of the Barrister exam is procedural and broad. You are tested on how disputes move through the system: who can do what, when, by what deadline, under which rule. There is a lot of procedure, and procedure means specifics that are easy to confuse under pressure.

Candidates from a litigation background, or those who did litigation-focused articling, often find the Barrister material more intuitive because they have seen how it works in practice.

## The Solicitor Exam

The Solicitor exam covers the transactional and advisory side: the work lawyers do outside of disputes, structuring deals, drafting, and advising on transactions. Subject areas generally include:

- Real estate
- Business law
- Wills, trusts, and estate administration
- Professional responsibility and practice management

The feel of the Solicitor exam is detail-heavy and precise. Real estate in particular is known for closing steps, timelines, and the mechanics of transactions, where the right answer turns on a specific requirement. Estate administration and business law similarly reward precision about process and sequence.

Candidates with transactional experience often prefer the Solicitor exam for the same reason litigators prefer the Barrister: they have seen the work.

## Which Is Harder?

There is no universal answer, and anyone who tells you one exam is definitively harder is generalizing from their own experience.

What is true is that the exams suit different strengths. If you are comfortable with procedure and breadth, the Barrister may feel more natural. If you are comfortable with detail and precision in a narrower set of transactional areas, the Solicitor may suit you.

Many candidates find the Solicitor exam's real estate content to be the most detail-intensive single area across both exams, which is why real estate gets a disproportionate amount of study time. But "most detailed single area" is not the same as "harder exam."

The honest framing: prepare for both seriously, and do not assume the one outside your comfort zone is the one that will trip you up. Sometimes familiarity breeds overconfidence, and candidates underprepare for the exam they assumed would be easy.

## Choosing Your Order

You can write the exams in either order and in the same cycle or different cycles. Considerations:

**Same cycle vs split.** Writing both in one cycle gets licensing done faster but means preparing a large volume of material at once. Splitting them lets you focus, but extends your timeline and means two separate preparation pushes.

**Your background.** Some candidates write the exam closer to their experience first to build confidence, then tackle the less familiar one. Others do the harder-for-them exam first to get it out of the way.

**Your schedule.** Articling, work, and life constraints often drive the decision more than strategy. That is fine. Pick the arrangement you can actually prepare for.

## How Preparation Should Differ

**For the Barrister:** Emphasize procedure and breadth. Build an index that handles the wide range of subject areas and the many procedural rules and deadlines. Practice questions that test "which rule, which deadline, which step" so you get fast at procedural distinctions.

**For the Solicitor:** Emphasize precision in detail-heavy areas, especially real estate. Know closing steps and transaction sequences cold. Your index needs to handle fine distinctions where the right answer turns on a specific requirement.

**For both:** Professional responsibility appears on each. Study it once, thoroughly, and it serves you on both exams.

## If You Pass One and Fail the Other

Each exam is scored independently. Passing one and failing the other is common and only requires retaking the one you did not pass. If this happens to you, it is not a referendum on your ability. It usually means the failed exam needed a different preparation emphasis, and a focused retake targeting that exam's specific demands often succeeds.

## The Bottom Line

The Barrister and Solicitor exams test different kinds of legal work and reward different preparation. Treat them as two distinct challenges rather than one big exam split in two. Match your study emphasis to each exam's texture, build a separate index tuned to each, and do not underprepare for the one you assume will be easy.
