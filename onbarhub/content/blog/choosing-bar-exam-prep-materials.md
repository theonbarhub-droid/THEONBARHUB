There is a lot of bar exam prep for sale, and candidates spend real money on it, often while stressed and unsure what they need. This guide gives you a clear way to judge any resource, so you buy what helps you pass and skip what just adds to the pile.

## Diagnose Before You Buy

The most expensive mistake is buying prep before you know what your problem is. Two candidates who are both struggling can need completely different things. One does not understand the material. One understands it but cannot find it fast enough. One knows it and finds it but runs out of time. One keeps misreading questions.

Each of those needs a different fix, and a product that solves one does nothing for another. Work out which is actually costing you marks first, from honest timed practice and review, and then let that decide what, if anything, you buy. Buying everything and hoping is how people spend a lot and improve little.

## Judge Quality, Not Volume

Prep is often sold on size: thousands of questions, hundreds of pages. Volume is the wrong measure.

What actually makes you better is the quality of the explanation. A question that tells you the right answer teaches you almost nothing. A question that tells you why the right answer is right, and why each wrong answer is wrong, and where the rule sits in the material, teaches you the thing you came for. You learn from the reasoning, not from the count.

So when you look at a practice resource, do not ask how many questions it has. Open a few and ask: when I get one wrong, does this show me exactly why, in a way that will help me next time I see something similar? If not, the size is irrelevant.

## Does It Train the Real Exam Skills?

The LSO exams are open-book, timed, and multiple choice. The skills that pass them are fast navigation, recognising what a question tests, pacing, and precise choosing between close answers. Good prep trains those skills, not just knowledge.

Ask of any resource: does it let me practise under real timed, open-book conditions? Does it help me build and test my index? Does it show me where my time goes and where I am losing marks? A product that only delivers facts, with no timed practice and no feedback on how you actually perform, is missing the part that matters most.

## Watch for What It Cannot Give You

Be realistic about what prep can and cannot do. No resource can learn the material for you, and none can guarantee a result. Be sceptical of anything that promises a specific outcome, and of anything that leans on urgency or fear to sell. The honest resources tell you that preparation lowers your risk and builds your skills, not that a purchase guarantees a pass.

Also be careful about the source and the licensing of any questions. Questions copied from past official exams or from other providers can carry rights issues, and material that is simply recycled from elsewhere often comes with the thin explanations that make it low value anyway. Original, well-explained material tends to be both safer and more useful.

## Build Your Own, Where You Can

Some of the most valuable prep is the kind you make yourself and cannot buy. Your personal index is the clearest example: nobody else's index will serve you as well as one built in your own words, organised the way your mind reaches for things. Your own log of the mistakes you keep making is another. Before you spend on products, make sure you are investing in the free, personal tools that often matter more.

## A Simple Test

Before you buy anything, ask four questions:

1. What specific problem of mine does this solve, and is that actually my problem?
2. When I get something wrong, does it teach me why, well?
3. Does it train the real exam skills, timed, open-book, with feedback?
4. Is there a free or self-made version that would do most of the same job?

If a resource passes that test, it is probably worth your money and time. If it fails, no amount of volume or marketing makes up for it.

## The Bottom Line

Do not buy prep to feel prepared. Diagnose your real gap first, then choose only what targets it. Judge resources by the quality of their explanations and whether they train the actual exam skills, not by how many questions they advertise. Be wary of guarantees, urgency, and recycled material, and remember that some of the most valuable preparation, your index and your own error log, is free and built by you.
