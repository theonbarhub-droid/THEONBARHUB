Failing an LSO licensing exam is more common than the silence around it suggests. Plenty of people who are now practising lawyers failed a sitting on the way. This guide explains what actually happens after a fail, what your options are, and how to come back and pass, without the panic.

First, the most important practical point: **the deadlines for your next attempt may arrive before you have fully processed the result.** Do not let the disappointment cost you the next window. Check the next sitting's deadlines immediately.

## The First 48 Hours

When a result comes back as a fail, the instinct is to spiral. Resist it long enough to do one concrete thing: **look up the deadlines for the next sitting of that exam.**

Here is why this is urgent. For the winter sitting, accommodation and registration deadlines can fall very close to, or even before, the release of the previous sitting's results. A candidate who fails the November Solicitor exam and wants to retake in March may find the accommodation deadline is within days of their result, and the registration deadline not long after.

If you need accommodations, that deadline is the one that bites first. Miss it and you may be able to register but not with the accommodations you need. So before you do anything else, confirm:

- The date of the next sitting
- Its registration deadline
- Its accommodation deadline, if you use accommodations

Get those on your calendar. Then you can take a breath.

## How Many Attempts You Get

The LSO sets limits on how many times you can attempt the licensing exams within the process, and there can be provisions around additional attempts in certain circumstances. These rules are set by LSO policy and can change.

Because this is consequential and subject to change, **confirm the current attempt rules directly with the LSO** rather than relying on forum posts or second-hand accounts. What someone experienced three years ago may not be the current policy. If you are approaching an attempt limit, contact the LSO to understand exactly where you stand and what options exist.

## Can You Appeal?

The exams are multiple-choice. That shapes what "appeal" means.

A multiple-choice exam is not re-marked the way a subjective essay might be reconsidered, because there is no marker judgment to revisit on each answer. If you believe there was a genuine error or irregularity in how your exam was administered or scored, the LSO is the place to raise it. But do not assume that requesting a review will convert a fail into a pass. For most candidates, the realistic path forward is preparing for and passing the next sitting, not contesting the result.

If something specific went wrong on exam day, a medical emergency, a test-centre problem, contact the LSO promptly about your options. There may be processes for genuine irregularities.

## Does It Go on Your Record?

No. A failed attempt does not appear on your license or in the public LSO directory once you are called. Employers looking you up do not see your attempt history. The fail is a private setback, not a permanent mark.

This matters psychologically. Many candidates catastrophize a fail as if it defines their career. It does not. Once you pass and are called, your license looks identical to everyone else's.

## Why Candidates Fail (and What It Usually Means)

Most fails are not about intelligence or legal ability. The common causes are specific and fixable:

**Running out of time.** The exams are tightly timed. Candidates who cannot locate answers fast enough leave questions unanswered. This is an index and practice problem, not a knowledge problem.

**Passive preparation.** Reading the materials without practicing questions leaves a gap between recognizing the material and being able to apply it under pressure.

**Underpreparing one area.** The Solicitor exam's real estate content, for example, is detail-heavy. Candidates who skimmed it pay for it.

**Weak navigation.** An untested or thin index means slow searching, which means running out of time.

The reason this is good news: these causes point to clear fixes. A targeted retake that addresses the specific cause of your fail has a strong chance of success.

## How to Come Back and Pass

A retake is not a repeat of your first attempt. You know the exam now. Use that.

**Diagnose honestly.** Was it time, a specific weak area, navigation, or genuine knowledge gaps? Your retake plan should target the actual cause, not just "study more."

**Fix your index.** If you ran out of time, your navigation system failed you. Rebuild it around how questions are framed and test it in timed mocks.

**Practice under time pressure.** If passive reading got you here, switch to active, timed practice. Train retrieval speed, not recognition.

**Target your weak area.** If one subject sank you, give it disproportionate attention this time.

**Mind the summer materials trap.** If your retake falls in a summer sitting, note that new study materials are released in April. A summer retake may use updated materials, so confirm you are studying the correct version.

## The Bottom Line

A failed LSO exam is a common, recoverable setback that does not appear on your license and does not define your career. The urgent task is protecting your next attempt by checking its deadlines immediately, especially accommodation deadlines, which can fall before your result is even released. The strategic task is diagnosing why you failed and building a targeted retake around that specific cause. Candidates who do both usually pass the next time.
